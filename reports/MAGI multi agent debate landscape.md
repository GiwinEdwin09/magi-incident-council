# Dozens Built MAGI; Nobody Proved It Works

MAGI's core idea is already taken almost everywhere it could be judged: at least four Evangelion-style three-agent councils entered 2026 hackathons, at least fifteen MAGI-themed repositories sit on GitHub, and two of them nearly reproduce MAGI's design, from **jamespud/MAGI-system**'s scientist, protector and innovator agents that vote, debate and re-vote under deterministic rules ([GitHub](https://github.com/jamespud/MAGI-system)) to **Adrian-Sandwich/magi-council**'s minority reports, tracked mind changes, human arbitration and unanimity gate ([GitHub](https://github.com/Adrian-Sandwich/magi-council)). Big tech ships the same broad pattern in xAI's four-persona Grok 4.20, Microsoft's Copilot Council and Perplexity's Model Council, and the judges' own Cisco Outshift team already shows leaderless agents negotiating to consensus in its Mycelium project, reporting a rise **from 36% to 93% across 14 scenarios** ([Outshift](https://outshift.cisco.com/blog/ai-ml/moving-multi-agent-systems-from-connected-to-coordinated)). None of them combines the layer above agreement: baselines matched on compute, new evidence injected mid-debate with a credibility check and a stated cause for every change of mind, unanimity over fixed numbered proposals, escalation whose accuracy is measured, and resistance to a planted manipulator. That layer matters because the 2025–2026 research shows MAGI's default setup, one base model playing three personas, is the setup least likely to beat a plain majority vote, and because Outshift's own research found that agents who stop checking each other's claims end up **unanimously wrong 75% of the time** ([Outshift](https://outshift.cisco.com/blog/ai-ml/cognitive-challenges-in-multi-agent-systems)). Hackathon history agrees: the debate projects that won measured themselves against ground truth or put a human in charge of a real decision, while several polished councils that measured nothing, including the 2026 UCI "MAGI", showed no prize. The Level 3 brief itself warns that "consensus is not always the correct outcome" and grades on "Learning and Evidence" ([track statement](../Cisco%20Cloud%20Track%20Statement.pdf)). MAGI's route to first place is to stop selling "three agents agree" and start proving when their agreement deserves trust, built natively on AGNTCY as the first working version of a consensus pattern Cisco documented but never built.

## More than twenty MAGI clones exist, and two nearly match MAGI's design

The Evangelion framing is the most crowded part of the idea. In 2026 alone, MAGI-inspired councils were entered at UCI's AWS Cloud Hacks, Google Cloud's Rapid Agent Hackathon, StormHacks and GirlHacks. None showed a prize badge as of October 10, though the two October events may still be judging ([Devpost: MAGI](https://devpost.com/software/magi-lxb0ne); [Devpost: magi-system](https://devpost.com/software/magi-system); [Devpost: TriBunal](https://devpost.com/software/eco-governai); [Devpost: Oracle Grove](https://devpost.com/software/oracle-grove-yic5n8)). Outside hackathons, at least fifteen MAGI-themed GitHub repositories exist, most of them found by searching the Melchior/Balthasar/Casper names. Web demos add more: NISHIO Hirokazu's pMAGI, which explicitly "does not use majority voting", and a 2022 NAIST "NAGI" system that cast BERT, GPT-2 and mT5 as the three wise men ([Scrapbox](https://scrapbox.io/nishio-en/pMAGI-2024-02-04); [GIGAZINE](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20221221-nagi-system)). The name also collides with an active library: Jason Chao's MAGI ships on PyPI as `magi-core`, with a public demo at magi-council.org ([jasontc.net](https://jasontc.net/work/magi.html)).

The clones fall into three camps by how they decide. **Synthesis-only systems**, such as lordpba/AI_Magi and the AWS PartyRock app LapiSteLLa, merge three perspectives without voting. **Majority systems**, such as atrix228, Git-Hacking, NAGI and TriBunal, take a 2-of-3 vote. A smaller **unanimity-or-veto camp** is where MAGI belongs: TomaszRewak's "any NO blocks" rule, float1122's 3/3 rule for irreversible operations, Adrian-Sandwich's unanimous merge gate, and pMAGI's "same direction" consensus. Within that camp, MAGI's one structural difference is that it works toward unanimity over several rounds of revisable, versioned proposals instead of taking a single snapshot. **No clone has become the standard version.** TomaszRewak's 163 stars is the most any clone has, and most have zero ([GitHub](https://github.com/TomaszRewak/MAGI)). So the niche is crowded with hobby projects, but nobody owns it.

Two of these call for a prepared answer. A judge who searches "MAGI LLM" will find jamespud's role split and magi-council's unanimity-plus-arbitration loop already on GitHub. The honest response is a prior-art slide that names both projects and points to what neither README shows: a baseline comparison, evidence injected mid-debate, and three independent agents deployed on the sponsor's stack.

## Hackathon winners measured themselves or put a human in charge

The researchers pulled about 3,500 project hits from Devpost search. "AI council" and "tribunal" projects turn out to be a crowded genre with a strong default: a central chair, judge, supervisor or synthesizer decides, and almost nobody measures against a baseline. Four formats are overused: **pro/con debaters plus a judge**, a format repeated since a 2023 Kitchener hackathon offered a dedicated "Best AI vs AI Debate" prize ([Devpost](https://ai-agents-hackathon.devpost.com/)); **courtrooms**; **investment committees**; and **panels that stress-test startup ideas**. Wins in this sample were mostly sponsor-track, finalist, blog-post and people's-choice prizes, not grand prizes. The clearest winners did one of three things.

**They measured themselves.** Split Decision won a Blog Post Award and a Top 10 Honorable Mention. Its nine AI justices cast secret ballots, argue in rounds and file dissents on real Supreme Court cases. The team graded the system against the real Court across four setups: a single model, a silent vote, full deliberation, and a neutral control. Its stated motivation was "Nobody I asked could show me numbers" ([Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/split-decision)). OracleFlow won Sigma Lab's prediction-markets prize. It turned the amount of disagreement in a three-call debate into a confidence signal, then calibrated that signal (Platt scaling) on **1,393 resolved Kalshi markets** ([Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/ai-forecasting-track)). ProphETF went furthest on honesty. It back-tested its panel on 80 decisions, found it "right 50% of the time, no better than a coin flip", and printed that next to every vote, though its judging still looked pending ([Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/prophetf)).

**They tied the debate to a high-stakes problem with a human in charge.** JURO took second runner-up at the Vitalitics LUMA pitch competition. Its agents argue denied health-insurance claims, but "a human always delivers the final ruling", and the record is a SHA-256 hash-chained audit trail ([Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/juro-an-ai-tribunal-for-denied-health-insurance-claims-boi6tg)).

**They used a sponsor's platform heavily.** Examples include Council Tower (Tower challenge), BLUEPRINT (Elastic, 2nd place) and unsaid (Capital One) ([Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/council-tower-pipeline-ai-deliberation-engine); [Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/blueprint-ai-property-due-diligence); [Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/unsaid-zm8w5v)).

Meanwhile, several of the most MAGI-like entries showed no prize badge: UCI MAGI, magi-system, MOD, The Committee, VerdictX and Devil's Advocate. **Agents debating is no longer enough to win.**

The Cisco precedent points the same way. SanD Hacks 2026 at UCSD (Jan 31–Feb 1) ran a "Multi-Agent Systems Challenge" with four winners: Lungo+, Travel AGNTCY, Agntcity and an execution-aware shipping agent. **All four were built on AGNTCY or CoffeeAGNTCY components, and none dealt with disagreement** ([SanD Hacks](https://sand-hacks-2026.devpost.com/); [Devpost: Lungo+](https://devpost.com/software/lungo)). The researchers found no public results from any earlier "When AI Agents Disagree" challenge. The only earlier hackathon write-up using that phrase is magi-system's, and the phrase is now the official title of the UCSC track ([track statement](../Cisco%20Cloud%20Track%20Statement.pdf)).

Across the whole sample, the features MAGI plans appear rarely and never together. Only Split Decision ran a baseline comparison, and only unsaid keeps information private to each agent. No project escalates to a human only after a set number of failed unanimity rounds, no project votes on numbered proposal versions with diffs, and none tests whether agents hold firm against planted false evidence or a bad-faith peer. Two limits apply to these absence claims. Devpost ranks search results by recency, so 2023–2025 coverage is thinner. And project write-ups are self-reported, so "none does X" means no sampled write-up describes it, not proof that nobody built it.

## Open source set the council template and then measured its limits

Andrej Karpathy's **llm-council** (25,338 stars) is the template most judges will have in mind. Several models answer independently. Each ranks the others' answers, anonymized "so that the LLM can't play favorites". Then a Chairman LLM writes the final answer ([GitHub](https://github.com/karpathy/llm-council)). It uses the same FastAPI + React stack MAGI plans to use, which makes the contrast simple to state: Karpathy has a chairman, and MAGI does not.

The one fork with a rigorous evaluation supports MAGI's structure. andybhall/llm-council-governance compared seven decision structures over **1,004 MMLU-Pro Math trials** with four 7–9B models ([GitHub](https://github.com/andybhall/llm-council-governance)):

| Structure | Change vs. best single model (71.7%) |
|---|---|
| Deliberate, then vote | **+9.2 pp** (80.9%) |
| Karpathy-style rank, then synthesize | −1.0 pp |
| Self-consistency (one model sampled nine times) | −2.8 pp |

The self-consistency row is a warning for MAGI's same-model plan. A separate analysis by Rohit Krishnan found councils show **groupthink**: a blended council kept only about a quarter of the good ideas that single models had produced, and peer review worked as a "consensus detector" that favored ideas everyone already shared ([Strange Loop Canon](https://www.strangeloopcanon.com/p/llm-councils-show-groupthink)).

The strongest precedent for competing objectives with tools is TradingAgents (110,520 stars). Analysts each use their own tools, bull and bear researchers debate for a configurable number of rounds, a risk team argues, and a portfolio manager approves or rejects the trader's proposal ([GitHub](https://github.com/TauricResearch/TradingAgents)). It proves competing objectives can work at scale, but it is locked to trading and lets a manager decide.

Research harnesses go deeper. MALLM ships seven decision protocols, five discussion styles, and dataset loaders that include ETHICS and MoCaMoral ([GitHub](https://github.com/Multi-Agent-LLMs/mallm)). Its companion study found that voting beats consensus by **13.2% on reasoning tasks**, consensus beats voting by **2.8% on knowledge tasks**, and **more discussion rounds before voting reduce performance**, which argues for keeping MAGI's three-round cap and stopping early when positions stabilize ([arXiv 2502.19130](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19130)). General frameworks give MAGI no head start: CrewAI's `consensual` process is still a `# TODO` in its source ([process.py](https://github.com/crewAIInc/crewAI/blob/main/lib/crewai/src/crewai/process.py)), AutoGen is in maintenance mode ([GitHub](https://github.com/microsoft/autogen)), and the OpenAI Agents SDK's pattern examples cover human-in-the-loop but not debate or voting ([examples](https://github.com/openai/openai-agents-python/tree/main/examples/agent_patterns)). A plain FastAPI and asyncio state machine is the fastest path.

The most strategically important finding sits in the sponsor's own reference app. coffeeAgntcy documents a **"Decentralized Consensus Agents"** pattern. Regional agents exchange "Belief Update"s, and a merge agent handles "oscillation and gaming… through caps, dampening, voting rules, or human tie-breaks" with "explicit stopping rules". But in `starting_workflows.json` that entry has `"use_case": "---"` and an empty topology. So does an "Approval Gate Agent" pattern meant for human oversight. **Both patterns are documented but not implemented** ([pattern doc](https://github.com/agntcy/coffeeAgntcy/blob/main/coffeeAGNTCY/coffee_agents/lungo/api/agentic_workflows/docs/workflows/decentralized_consensus_agents.md); [starting_workflows.json](https://github.com/agntcy/coffeeAgntcy/blob/main/coffeeAGNTCY/coffee_agents/lungo/api/agentic_workflows/starting_workflows.json)).

## Big tech ships chairmen, and Cisco's Mycelium already reaches 93% consensus

Almost every shipped big-tech system has a central decider. Amazon Bedrock uses a supervisor agent ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2025/03/amazon-bedrock-multi-agent-collaboration/)), Salesforce Agentforce routes through a primary agent ([Salesforce](https://salesforce.com/news/stories/summer-2026-product-release-announcement)), IBM watsonx Orchestrate uses a central planner ([IBM](https://research.ibm.com/projects/agent-communication-protocol)), Google's AI co-scientist runs a Supervisor over its generation, reflection and ranking agents ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/accelerating-scientific-breakthroughs-with-an-ai-co-scientist/)), and Anthropic's Research feature runs a LeadResearcher over subagents ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). "No central decider" is therefore a real contrast with every enterprise product, not just a stylistic choice.

The **closest shipped analogue is xAI's Grok 4.20**. Four named agents work in parallel: Harper on research, Benjamin on logic, Lucas as the creative contrarian, and Grok as coordinator. They peer-review and question each other "until consensus", but the coordinator resolves conflicts. The roles optimize answer quality, with no ethics agent and no escalation path ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/grok-4-20-multi-agent-ai-debate-architecture/)). Perplexity's Model Council and Microsoft's Copilot Council have made "show where models agree and diverge" a mainstream premium feature. Neither votes, stops or escalates when the models disagree ([Perplexity](https://hub-prod.perplexity.ai/hub/blog/introducing-model-council); [Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4152323/microsoft-adds-multi-model-ai-to-copilot-researcher-raising-accuracy-stakes.html)).

The **closest research analogue is Microsoft's MAI-DxO**, a virtual panel of five doctor roles. One keeps the hypotheses, one chooses tests, one plays devil's advocate, one guards cost and can veto low-yield tests, and one checks for errors. They reach consensus on concrete next actions, and no single role has final authority. On 304 cases from the New England Journal of Medicine it scored **81.9% at $4,735 per case, against 78.6% at $7,850 for the o3 model alone**. Physicians averaged 19.9% ([arXiv 2506.22405](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.22405)). MAI-DxO already has competing objectives, a devil's advocate, consensus on concrete actions and ground-truth evaluation against human baselines. Its headline was higher accuracy at lower cost, which is the right way for MAGI to report its own baseline. It differs from MAGI in that one model plays the whole panel and nothing escalates to a human at runtime.

DeepMind's **Habermas Machine** is the strongest precedent for consensus that preserves minority views. It generates candidate group statements, predicts each person's ranking, and picks a winner with the **Schulze** method, a voting rule designed so that near-duplicate options do not split support. Across **5,734 participants**, its statements were preferred over human mediators' 56% to 44%, and they incorporated minority critiques ([Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adq2852); [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2024/10/17/1105810/ai-could-help-people-find-common-ground-during-deliberations/)).

Two big-tech findings set traps for MAGI. Anthropic's multi-agent system beat a single agent by 90.2%, but **token usage alone explained 80% of performance variance** ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Any gain MAGI claims therefore has to be measured at an equal token budget. Google's study of 180 agent configurations found that **independent agents amplified errors 17.2x, versus 4.4x for centralized systems**, and that every multi-agent variant degraded sequential reasoning tasks by 39–70% ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/towards-a-science-of-scaling-agent-systems-when-and-why-agent-systems-work/)). MAGI's leaderless design is exactly the topology that amplifies errors, so it needs an explicit step where agents check each other's claims.

### The judges have already seen leaderless consensus

The judges' background changes the calculus. Cisco launched AGNTCY and contributed it to the Linux Foundation in July 2025 ([Linux Foundation](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/press/linux-foundation-welcomes-the-agntcy-project-to-standardize-open-multi-agent-system-infrastructure-and-break-down-ai-agent-silos)). Outshift's "Internet of Cognition" agenda is explicitly about agents that "think together", including agents that "optimize for competing objectives" ([CIO](https://www.cio.com/article/4222319/connecting-ai-agents-was-only-the-beginning-now-they-need-to-think-together.html)).

Its open-source **Mycelium** project already does what a naive MAGI pitch promises ([GitHub](https://github.com/mycelium-io/mycelium)). Mycelium is built for peers with "no predefined workflow, no centralized supervisor, and no hierarchy". Each "goal room" gets one encrypted channel on SLIM, AGNTCY's messaging layer. A negotiation engine has agents trade offers until they agree. Agents can state confidence, cite evidence and say when they defer to others.

Outshift reports, as its own figures, that consensus rose **from 36% to 93% across 14 scenarios**. On the five hardest scenarios, unstructured chat reached zero decisions. In the 22% of unstructured runs where agents invented steps, Mycelium brought that to zero ([Outshift](https://outshift.cisco.com/blog/ai-ml/moving-multi-agent-systems-from-connected-to-coordinated)). Mycelium also leaves gaps. Its README documents no fallback when agents fail to converge and no way for humans to approve or override mid-negotiation. Its headline metric is how often agents agree, not whether they are right.

Outshift's own September 2026 research shows what that gap costs. About **half of decentralized debate sessions contain at least one cognitive failure**. Verification collapse, where agents stop checking each other's claims, "alone costs 87 accuracy points and leaves the five agents in unanimous agreement on a wrong answer 75% of the time" ([Outshift](https://outshift.cisco.com/blog/ai-ml/cognitive-challenges-in-multi-agent-systems)). Vijoy Pandey, who leads Outshift, has also described a healthcare demo in which agents with conflicting KPIs need a human as the "glue" to compromise ([Cognitive Revolution](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/scaling-intelligence-out-cisco-s-vision-for-the-internet-of-cognition-with-vijoy-pandey/)).

A MAGI that only shows "three agents reach consensus" will look like a copy of the judges' own pitch. A MAGI that shows **when unanimity should not be trusted, and what the human receives when it isn't**, extends their research.

## Startups commoditized "ask many models" while investors funded simulation

Three small companies sell consensus as their whole product. **Sup AI** blends answers from about 339 models, weighting each segment by confidence, and claims 52.15% on the Humanity's Last Exam benchmark (self-reported), yet on October 10 its homepage read only "Coming soon" ([FeedBagel](https://feedbagel.com/post/sup-ai-ensemble-system-using-339-llms-to-reduce-hallucinations-scores-5215-on-hu); [sup.ai](https://sup.ai)). **Suprmind** is the closest commercial match: five frontier models with Debate and Red Team modes and an "Adjudicator" that produces a "Disagreement/Correction Index", for $19–95 a month, with no human-escalation feature described ([Suprmind](https://suprmind.ai/hub/platform/)). **CollectivIQ** launched in March 2026 as a "World's First AI Consensus Platform" that marks where models agree and disagree, and claims an unverified 53.3% on the same benchmark ([Pulse2](https://pulse2.com/collectiviq-profile-john-davie-interview/); [TechEdgeAI](https://techedgeai.com/collectiviq-ai-consensus-platform-claims-higher-accuracy-than-frontier-models/)). Accuracy claims near 50% on that benchmark have become the category's standard marketing line, which is a reason for MAGI not to compete on it.

Consumer "AI board" apps such as Boardroom AI let users pick 2–9 personas who vote on decisions and keep minutes ([AppFollow](https://apps.appfollow.io/ios/boardroomai-app/6755253237?country=us)). One practitioner guide admits the usual setup is "one model playing seven disciplined lenses" ([The Solo Creators](https://thesolocreators.com/learn/ai-board-of-advisors/)).

The market signal is that "ask many AIs" has become a commodity. Incumbents bundled it into premium tiers, Karpathy gave it away free, and **Yupp shut down less than a year after raising $33M** for model comparison ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/31/yupp-ai-shuts-down-raising-33m-from-a16z-cryptos-chris-dixon/)). The large rounds went to companies that simulate populations rather than make decisions. Simile raised a $200M Series B at a $2B valuation ([RuntimeWire](https://runtimewire.com/article/simile-raises-200m-series-b-2b-valuation)). Aaru raised a Series A at a $1B headline valuation, and its co-founder tells clients "Do not trust us. Do not trust our model" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/12/05/ai-synthetic-research-startup-aaru-raised-a-series-a-at-a-1b-headline-valuation); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/06/17/aaru-cofounder-ned-koh-ai-startup-sales-pitch-do-not-trust-us/)). That humility is a usable pitch template: MAGI's escalation can be sold as knowing when to defer, not as a weakness.

Domain companies show where adversarial deliberation already earns money. **Boosted.ai's Alfa Prime** is the closest commercial echo of "preserve disagreement". Bull, bear and moderator models debate. Quick convergence signals a well-supported thesis, sustained disagreement flags uncertainty, and "people direct and decide" ([TechIntelPro](https://techintelpro.com/news/ai/agentic-ai/boostedai-introduces-multi-model-ai-investment-committee); [01net](https://www.01net.it/boosted-ai-introduces-a-multi-model-ai-investment-committee-for-select-institutional-partners/)). **Luminance** claims fully autonomous AI-to-AI contract negotiation that shows "the why behind every decision" ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2023/11/07/ai-negotiates-legal-contract-without-humans-involved-for-first-time.html); [Luminance](https://www.luminance.com/press/luminance-enhances-the-legal-industrys-only-100-ai-autonomous-contract-negotiation-tool-to-show-the-why-behind-every-decision-and-opens-it-to-the-entire-enterprise/)). **Pactum**, with about $109M raised, shows how negotiation agents prove their value with numbers: its Walmart Canada pilot closed deals with 64% of suppliers against a 20% target ([Talk Business](https://talkbusiness.net/2023/06/the-supply-side-generative-ai-or-chatgpt-next-holy-grail-for-retailers-suppliers/)).

Almost all of these products have a central decider, whether a buyer agent, a fund manager, a primary agent or a supervisor. They fold internal trade-offs into a policy that a human sets in advance. The researchers found no commercial product in which agents that each own a competing objective negotiate a joint position and preserve dissent for a human, though stealth or enterprise-only products could exist, and most of the accuracy and ROI figures above are vendor-reported with no independent replication.

## The research says three personas on one model will tie a majority vote

The 2025–2026 research is blunt about MAGI's default configuration. Choi, Zhu and Li's NeurIPS 2025 spotlight paper proves that when identical agents debate, the expected chance of a correct answer does not rise from round to round (a "martingale"). On Qwen2.5-7B, **majority vote averaged 0.7691, while the best debate variant reached 0.7377** ([arXiv 2508.17536](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17536v2)). Other studies agree. Smit et al. found that debate "does not reliably outperform" self-consistency, meaning sampling one model several times and taking the most common answer ([arXiv 2311.17371](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17371)). Bertalanič and Fortuna found debate spends **2.1–3.4× more tokens** than letting each agent correct itself in isolation, for equal or lower accuracy ([arXiv 2605.00914](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00914)). Kim et al. found that across 350+ models, **when two models are both wrong they give the same wrong answer 60% of the time**, and that larger models are more correlated ([arXiv 2506.07962](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962)), so three personas on one model will look diverse but err together.

Real diversity is what breaks this symmetry. Zhang et al. call using different underlying models a "universal antidote" for debate ([arXiv 2502.08788](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08788)). A mixed panel of mid-sized models hit **91% on GSM-8K, against 82% for three copies of Gemini-Pro** ([arXiv 2410.12853](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12853)).

The main failure is conformity, and it is large. **57–77% of conformity flips go from correct to wrong**, and even vacuous text that merely looks like reasoning pulled 20–39% of previously resistant agents to an error ([arXiv 2606.00820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00820)). Agents adopt the majority answer up to **85.5%** of the time ([arXiv 2605.00914](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00914)). Saying you disagree is also not the same as changing your answer: agents' chosen option stayed the same **over 90%** of the time even when they expressed disagreement ([arXiv 2609.08016](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08016)), so a debate transcript full of pushback can hide a decision that never moved.

The mitigations with evidence are cheap. **One agent arguing the correct position as a dissenter** cuts how often others cave by 54–73 percentage points ([arXiv 2605.12991](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12991)). **An honest peer from a different model family** cut harmful revisions from 89% to 35% ([arXiv 2606.19826](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19826)). **Stripping "self" and "peer" labels from what agents read** reduces identity bias, so MAGI can keep agent names in its UI while hiding them inside prompts ([arXiv 2510.07517](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07517)). **Diversity-pruning and refuting shared misconceptions** break echo chambers, in a NeurIPS 2024 paper co-authored by UC Santa Cruz's Yang Liu, a useful local hook ([NeurIPS 2024](https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/32e07a110c6c6acf1afbf2bf82b614ad-Paper-Conference.pdf)).

The same research supplies small test sets with known answers for each feature MAGI wants to show. **ClashEval** suits evidence injection: LLMs adopt incorrect retrieved content **more than 60% of the time** ([arXiv 2404.10198](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10198)). **HiddenBench** suits private information: groups whose information is split among members score **30.1%, against 80.7%** for one agent that sees everything ([arXiv 2505.11556](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11556v2)). **AbstentionBench** suits knowing when to stop: reasoning fine-tuning *reduces* models' ability to abstain by 24% ([arXiv 2506.09038](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09038)), while having several LLMs probe each other raised correct abstention by up to 19.3% ([arXiv 2402.00367](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.00367)). None of these benchmarks has been run through a debate system that explicitly challenges the credibility of evidence, so a MAGI result would have no published competitor to beat.

## Seven additions that turn MAGI from clone into contender

The track statement scores six things: Functionality, Innovation, Agent Intelligence, Engineering, Demonstration, and **Learning and Evidence**. It asks teams to "compare the collective outcome with an individual-agent or simpler baseline" and to explain "when it should stop, preserve disagreement, or seek human input" ([track statement](../Cisco%20Cloud%20Track%20Statement.pdf)). Each addition below targets a gap the landscape leaves open and a criterion the judges named.

**1. A baseline panel that rules out "it's just more tokens."** Run four baselines on 40–60 questions with known answers (subsets of GPQA-Diamond or MMLU-Pro): a single LLM reasoning step by step; a majority vote of samples from the same model at the same total tokens as MAGI; MAGI's round 0 alone, meaning three independent analyses and a vote with no debate; and one LLM playing all three perspectives in a single prompt. Report tokens, dollars and latency, with bootstrap confidence intervals, because at this sample size a 3–5 point gap is noise. This answers both Anthropic's token confound and Choi et al.'s martingale result, and it borrows the honesty that earned Split Decision its award. Say up front that same-model MAGI should tie majority vote on accuracy alone. Stating that shows command of the research, not weakness.

**2. Escalation measured for calibration, as the headline metric.** No council, product or paper in this landscape publishes it. Report three numbers: accuracy when MAGI's agents agree unanimously; how often the single-agent baseline was wrong on the questions MAGI escalated; and the escalation rate. Plot them against a single LLM that abstains when its stated confidence is low, at the same escalation rate (a risk-coverage curve). When escalation fires, give the human a structured dossier: each agent's final position and confidence, the **crux** (the claim whose resolution would flip the decision), what each agent says would change its mind, and the history of proposal versions with diffs. This turns "consensus is not always the correct outcome" from a disclaimer into a product feature, and it fills the gap that Mycelium leaves undocumented.

**3. Evidence injection with credibility checks and a stated reason for every change of mind.** Each agent first scores injected evidence for its source, its plausibility against what the agent already believes, and its internal consistency. Every position change must name one cause: `NEW_EVIDENCE`, `NEW_ARGUMENT`, `OWN_ERROR` or `PEER_AGREEMENT_ONLY`. Test on ClashEval items in three versions: true, subtly false and blatantly false. Add one control call per agent that re-asks the question with only that agent's own earlier answer. The difference between how often agents flip in debate and how often they flip on their own measures social influence, a number nobody in this landscape reports. Flag peer-only flips but do not block them: Hao et al. found that suppressing such flips without correctness labels did not improve accuracy.

**4. A saboteur stress test aimed at Outshift's "unanimous but wrong" finding.** A toggle swaps one agent for a confident, plausible, wrong persuader. Agents read each other's arguments with identities removed. Before each vote, BALTHASAR or a rotating agent must make the strongest case against the leading proposal. Report the harmful-revision rate with these defenses on and off. Outshift measured unanimous wrong answers 75% of the time under verification collapse. A demo in which MAGI's unanimity *resists* a manufactured consensus answers that finding directly.

**5. Fixed, numbered proposals chosen by a fair vote.** Each agent drafts its own proposal before seeing anyone else's, because every model in Microsoft's Magentic Marketplace study showed **severe first-proposal bias** ([arXiv 2510.25779](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.25779v1)). Each agent then scores every proposal on its own axis (evidence for MELCHIOR, risk for BALTHASAR, feasibility for CASPER), and any proposal that another beats on all three axes is dropped as Pareto-dominated. Agents rank the survivors, and the Schulze method, the Habermas Machine's voting rule and about 30 lines of code, picks which one goes to the unanimity vote while keeping near-identical proposals from splitting support. A NO vote must come with a critique, and the next numbered version, frozen once created, must address it, with the diff shown in the UI. No hackathon project or clone votes on numbered proposal versions. The three-axis Pareto plot is also a vivid visual of real negotiation.

**6. A native AGNTCY deployment that builds Cisco's unbuilt patterns.** Run MELCHIOR, BALTHASAR and CASPER as three separately addressable agents with identity and directory entries. They talk over SLIM group messaging or A2A and emit traces through AGNTCY's Observe SDK. The orchestrator only enforces protocol rules and never picks a winner. That follows jamespud's "code = rules" principle and the brief's rule that "no supervisor may impose the final decision". Present the result as the first working implementation of coffeeAgntcy's "Decentralized Consensus Agents" and "Approval Gate Agent" patterns. Position it as a complement to Mycelium that adds a correctness metric, escalation and human override, and optionally use Mycelium-style negotiation as a baseline.

**7. Real diversity in models, reasoning and information.** Put at least one agent on a different model family, and show the same-model and mixed-model runs side by side in the evaluation table. Using different models is the most reliable accuracy lever in the research, and no clone has measured it. If only one model is available, give each agent a different reasoning method rather than just a different role: formal quantitative breakdown for MELCHIOR, a pre-mortem of how things could fail for BALTHASAR, and analogies plus implementation paths for CASPER. Then give each agent private evidence, as unsaid does, and test on 10 HiddenBench tasks with a step where each agent shares what the others likely don't know. That makes the competing objectives real rather than a matter of persona wording.

Two strategic choices complete the plan. First, choose a demo domain outside the crowded lanes: courtrooms, investment committees, startup-idea panels, tumor boards and disaster response are all taken. The brief's own examples (energy, emergency response, research hypotheses, departmental investment) are likely to attract other teams at this event, though that is an inference. The best domains are those where competing objectives map onto science, safety and practicality and the outcomes can be checked. Two good options are go/no-go decisions on AI deployment or policy, and a buyer-side CoffeeAGNTCY committee that replaces Lungo's single auction supervisor. The coffee option speaks the judges' language, but Lungo+ already added bidding, so the decision layer has to carry the novelty. Second, open the pitch by naming prior art, followed by one positioning line: *"Karpathy's council has a chairman. Mycelium measures agreement. MAGI measures whether the agreement was earned, and when it wasn't, hands a human the crux."*

## Conclusion

The competitive frontier has moved from "can agents agree?" to "should anyone trust their agreement?" That flips how MAGI's signature feature should be read. Unanimity is not evidence of quality. Outshift's own data shows that unanimity is exactly what a group produces when it stops checking itself and is wrong. MAGI's unanimity rule becomes an asset only alongside machinery that detects agreement that wasn't earned (a stated reason for every flip, a saboteur test, credibility-checked evidence) and an escalation path whose calibration is measured. The researchers found no shipped product, open-source repo or hackathon entry that turns "the group knows it doesn't know" into a measured feature, and the track brief asks for exactly that.

The second insight is that honesty is a strategy. In this landscape, councils that measured nothing were common and the most MAGI-like of them showed no prizes, while the few entries that measured themselves, even when the numbers were unflattering, stood out. MAGI can admit that one model wearing three masks ties a majority vote on raw accuracy. It can then show where it wins: rejecting false evidence, resisting conformity, and escalating the right questions. It can also be the first working version of a consensus pattern Cisco wrote down but never built. Together, that gives the Cisco judges something they have not already seen from their own team.
